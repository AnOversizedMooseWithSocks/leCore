leCore — cloth

A 40×40 sheet with 9,202 constraints — structural, shear and bend — solved by substepped position-based dynamics entirely on the GPU. Grab it and throw it around. Every constraint scatters a correction to both endpoints by additive blending; each particle applies the average; repeat. The page checks its own solver against a CPU reference on load and reports whether the constraint residual is actually falling.

What this is, and what it is not. As of 2026 three.js ships an official WebGPU compute cloth example, and there are several mature WebGPU XPBD cloth simulators in the wild (jspdown/cloth, ccincotti3/webgpu_cloth_simulator, nakjun/Cloth-Simulation-WebGPU). WebGPU has roughly 95% browser coverage since Safari 26. This page is not a better cloth simulator than any of those, and it would be silly to claim so — a compute shader with atomics is the right tool for this job and three.js gives you a renderer as well.

What it demonstrates is narrower and, I think, more interesting. The standard reference on the subject says WebGL 2 has no compute stage, so WebGL-only projects fall back to encoding state in float textures and updating them with full-screen fragment passes — and that this fallback “can’t do arbitrary writes”. This page does arbitrary writes anyway. Each constraint emits a point primitive from the vertex stage, positioned onto the particle it corrects, and additive blending sums the corrections in hardware. That is a scatter-add without a compute shader, and it is what lets a solver — not just an update rule — run on the WebGL 2 fallback path. leCore uses the same primitive for its inverted-index scorer and its resonator; cloth is the version of it you can grab.

The other difference is bookkeeping, not graphics: this page checks its own solver. On load it runs the GPU solver and the same Jacobi scheme in JavaScript from an identical stretched state, compares every particle, pulls one particle out of place to confirm the constraints actually resist, and reports all of it above. The kernel it runs is the one in leCore’s catalog, whose verification note carries its measured numbers: 2.407e-06 relative against NumPy and 51× faster on an RTX A4500, with the constraint residual falling identically on both paths.

Substeps, not iterations, are what make it stiff. Measured against a NumPy reference before this page was written: one substep with 40 iterations leaves a relative residual of 0.114 — worse than 16 iterations — while 6×5 gives 0.051. A Jacobi correction travels one cell per iteration, so a 40-wide sheet would need ~40 iterations for a pinned edge to be felt at the far corner; substepping sidesteps that by never letting the cloth get far from satisfied. Damping is per frame and the per-substep factor is its root — applying it once per substep compounds it and the cloth looks dead.
